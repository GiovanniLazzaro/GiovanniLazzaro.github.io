Imagine you’re a young PhD student. After an exhausting week spent juggling classes, seminars, endless data collection, and hours wrestling with prompts on Claude, the weekend is finally here. You leave the department and rush to grab a cold beer with your colleagues.

At the table, the complaints are the usual academic litany: "Mario left research after ten years of precarious contracts," "Only one in ten makes it to professor," "My supervisor has been ghosting me for weeks," "I don't understand this statistics stuff at all," "I always feel like the least intelligent person in the room." Culminating in the inevitable, resigned shrug: "Who cares, just have Claude write it."

Meanwhile, one beer turns into two. Actually, make it three.

You go home and, as soon as you hit the bed, you collapse into a deep sleep. Or so you think. When you open your eyes, the air smells of fixatives and ink. No screens, no notifications, no publish-or-perish anxiety. You’ve been catapulted back to the late 19th century. Neuroscience is still an unexplored wasteland, the very shape of neurons is a hotly debated mystery, and there are still people around who take phrenology seriously.

But you’re in luck: you’ve ended up in the laboratory of Santiago Ramón y Cajal.

Working by his side means getting your hands dirty in the most literal sense. Glass slides, reagents, hand-cut sections. Research is a marathon of pure physical and mental effort. There are no PDFs to download with a click from Sci-Hub: you wait months for the few available publications. Yet, in this total lack of convenience, something unexpected happens. This grueling, forced slowness compels you to think deeply about what you are doing. With no shortcuts, every conceptual doubt can only be resolved in one way: through stubborn, repeated, and exhausting observation.

You knock timidly on the laboratory door, which opens with a dull, creaking sound. No one is inside, so you take the opportunity to snoop around. Among the many tomes and notes scattered on the table, you stumble upon a manuscript titled Advice for a Young Investigator.

"This is exactly what I needed," you think. You light a small lamp, get comfortable, and begin to read.

Leafing through those pages, you discover that Cajal's advice for aspiring young scientists seems tailor-made to answer your Friday night pub complaints:

The myth of the genius: Cajal deeply despised the idea that science was an exclusive reserve for elite minds. He believed, on the contrary, that excessive admiration for "geniuses" only created a massive inferiority complex (self-doubt) capable of paralyzing young researchers and extinguishing their initiative.

The disease of the will: For the neuroscientist, true failure almost never stemmed from a lack of innate intelligence, but from what he called the "disease of the will." Laziness, distraction, lack of focus, and the inability to persist on a problem are the real enemies of discovery.

The praise of slowness: Those who learn slowly and struggle immensely to master concepts often internalize them more deeply and solidly than those who grasp them in an instant, only to forget them just as quickly.

You wake up abruptly from this dream, your head spinning and a question echoing in your mind: does Cajal's advice still make sense in 2026?

Over the last three years, AI has seen growth that is an understatement to call exponential. Even as I write these words, Anthropic has released Fable 5, and new repositories pop up daily on GitHub to build autonomous agents capable of replacing a large chunk of our practical research work.

The questions, at this point, overwhelm you. Will AI replace us, or will it finally free up our time to think? Will we become slaves to the algorithm, or will we simply specialize in controlling information flows? In such a landscape, how does the role of the PhD student change?

If AI allows us to reach any goal in a fraction of the time, who truly stands out? Does the fastest always win? Will having all the information ready and packaged without any effort lead us to catch that "disease of the will" that terrified Cajal?

In short, what advice would Cajal give to a young researcher in the 2026? What makes us really skilled at doing science when technical competencies have been automated? Who wins: the one who can write the best prompt to control the code, the one with the most brilliant initial intuition, or the one who manages to use AI not as a shortcut, but as a tool for solid, lasting learning?
